# Frameworks: Nick Kokonas

Built from notes on 12 public sources (2011 to 2026), with quotes checked against the live pages.

13 frameworks, most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources; S010 and S011 are the same essay and count as one (written S010/S011). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are guidance for the council built from his stated numbers, not quotes. The Fit lines flag where his experience (a sold-out, prepaid tasting-menu destination and a venture-backed reservation software company) transfers badly to a neighborhood or casual operator.

---

## 1. Tuesday is not Saturday (two-direction, time-slot pricing)
- **What it is:** The same seat is a different product at different times, so it should carry a different price. Price moves down in slow slots as well as up in busy ones. A price that only rises is a surge fee, breaks the fairness norm, and makes customers cry foul.
- **Parts:** Map demand by day and hour. Lower the price of the slow slot (Alinea at 5 pm Wednesday ran roughly $80 to $100 a person below 8 pm Saturday, S001). Raise it where demand exceeds supply. Reset prices on a fast time scale, daily or hourly, not once a quarter on a printed menu (S006). Call it dynamic, not surge (S006). It applies to any time-slotted business: dentists, salons, trainers (S002, S004, S009).
- **When to use:** Menu and ticket pricing, happy hours and daypart offers, event and private-room pricing, any slot that sells out while another sits empty.
- **How to score:** 5 if the plan lowers price in empty slots as well as charging more at peak, and the operator has slot-level demand data. 3 if price varies only through a fixed happy hour. 1 if it only raises peak prices or adds a cover charge.
- **Fit:** He says pricing cannot be pressed hard when demand is below supply (S004). For a casual bar the useful half is usually the downward half, filling the dead daypart; raising Saturday prices on regulars carries a risk his destination never faced. Inferred from S004, S005, S010.
- **Quote:** "Tuesday is not Saturday" (S004); "Pricing should move in two directions" (S010)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S002, S003, S004, S005, S006, S009, S010/S011, S017)

## 2. Free, deposit, prepay by demand *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Match the booking type to how scarce the seat is, and decide it per day and per experience, not once for the whole restaurant.
- **Parts:** Supply above demand: free reservation. Demand above supply: small deposit. Demand extreme (rare): full prepayment. A chef's counter can be prepaid on a fixed template while the main room stays free and flexes (S011). Put the scarce seat (counter by the oven, best corner table) behind a $10 to $20 deposit and keep the rest free (S004, S005). Credit the deposit to the bill so it never reads as a fee (S017).
- **When to use:** Deciding whether to take deposits, which nights or rooms get them, holiday and event booking policy.
- **How to score:** 5 if the paid tier covers only slots that already sell out and the deposit is credited to the check. 3 if a deposit covers every weekend booking without slot data. 1 if a deposit goes on every booking at a place that rarely fills.
- **Fit:** His own numbers say the paid share is small: free was about 70 percent of Tock volume (S004), about 80 percent (S005), and 98 percent of bookings at mid-price restaurants (S010). For a neighborhood place the ladder is a peak-night and holiday tool, not a default.
- **Quote:** "When supply exceeds demand, reservations should be free." (S010)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S004, S005, S009, S010/S011, S012, S017)

## 3. Deposit as sunk cost (skin in the game) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A guest with any money in shows up; a free reservation is mentally priced at zero and easy to drop. He found it in his own behavior: movie tickets he did not want to use but used anyway (S001, S003).
- **Parts:** Take a small amount the guest can easily afford ($5 to $10). Take real money, not a card on file plus a cancellation fee, which feels like a penalty and does not work (S005, S012). Count partial seatings and very late cancels as no-shows or the stats flatter you (S012). Tock's 2017 data: ordinary reservations 8.04 percent no-show, deposit 3.67, prepaid 0.89 (S012).
- **When to use:** No-show and short-seat problems on busy nights, Valentine's Day and other holidays (up to 35 percent of diners making multiple bookings, S012), large-party bookings.
- **How to score:** Guidance: estimate no-shows plus short seats as a share of peak-night revenue. He treats 5 to 8 percent of nightly revenue as a serious leak (S001) and says 5 percent absent can wipe out a thin bottom line (S017). Above that, a deposit scores high; near zero, it adds friction for nothing.
- **Fit:** Walk-in bars and counter-service spots rarely have this leak. He does argue a booking can help walk-in places whose guests stay away fearing waits (S017), but that is a 2016 promotional claim.
- **Quote:** "once they had $5 in for their reservation as a deposit, it took the no-show rate from 14 percent to under three percent." (S003); "Holding a credit card and charging after the fact simply does not work." (S012)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S003, S004, S005, S009, S012, S017)

## 4. Find the mispricing: why-questions, read the rules, every arbitrage is a time scale *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Conventions persist because nobody asks why, and opacity is where margin hides. An arbitrage exists because some step is slow; find the slow step, build the faster path, and take the gap a little at a time, because every arb closes.
- **Parts:** Ask why a convention exists (host stands, phone reservations, publisher advances, S001). Ask the vendor why a price is what it is: a beef quote fell from $34 to $18 a pound on a four-month prepay once he learned the aging cycle (S004). Find the true cost of goods (about $2.23 a book on a print run for a book retailing near $60, S001). Read the actual regulations; most people and their lawyers go by habit (S006). Count a competitor's claimed clients yourself (S011). Ask who benefits from the old system (hosts taking cash tips to hold tables, S004, S005). Aim for small, high hoops: fields with a scary failure rate, few players, and a good prize (S001, S006).
- **When to use:** Vendor negotiations, any industry norm treated as fixed, platform contracts, a pitch that rests on "that is how it is done".
- **How to score:** High if the founder can name the slow step or hidden cost and the evidence they checked. Low if the plan copies industry practice without knowing why it exists.
- **Fit:** Transfers well at any size. The trading-floor arbitrages are dated, and he says one that still worked would not be discussed on a podcast (S006).
- **Quote:** "Nothing's priced forever." (S006); "I always try to look for the high, small hoops." (S001)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S004, S005, S006, S010/S011, S012)

## 5. Last-dollar economics (every empty seat is an expiring option) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** On a fixed cost base the last money in is the money you keep. An unfilled seat expires worthless, so incremental revenue in a slow slot mostly drops to the bottom line, and small recurring leaks add up to a large annual number.
- **Parts:** Know the split: labor 30 to 35 percent, food 30 to 35, fixed and overhead about 30, leaving about 10 (S004). Extra revenue with no new staff or rent drops through at about 80 percent (S009). Multiply any small loss by seven days and 52 weeks (S001, S004). Sell an unfilled seat at half price, or even below food cost, for incremental revenue (S004, S005).
- **When to use:** Slow-daypart offers, no-show fixes, deciding whether a promotion pays, margin targets.
- **How to score:** Guidance: annualize the leak or the gain. His Alinea no-show cost is stated as about $580,000 a year (S009) and as over a million dollars a year (S004); his margin claims range from 6 to 8 percent rising to 20 to 25 at peak (S009), 10 to 12 rising to 20 to 25 (S001), and 7 to 8 rising to 18 to 20 (S006). Use the range, not one figure.
- **Fit:** The 80 percent drop-through assumes salaried, fixed labor. A casual operator who staffs to volume keeps less of each extra dollar. Selling below food cost also sits in tension with his warning that free attracts people who need free (S004). Inferred from S004, S009.
- **Quote:** "the last money in is the money you keep" (S009); "looking everything as like an expiring option" (S005)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S004, S005, S006, S009, S017)

## 6. Know what you are selling (the Gramercy Tavern test)
- **What it is:** List every distinct thing a venue sells and make each one choosable before the guest arrives. People cannot buy what they cannot see.
- **Parts:** List the products: tavern bar, a la carte room, tasting menus, private rooms, cookbook, merchandise (S004, S005). Expose each as a bookable choice. Show real inventory, not "available or not", or guests phone anyway (S017, S001). Put add-ons (wine pairings, books) in the booking flow, not at the end of the meal where they feel like an upsell (S001). Choose the booking mix per experience (S011).
- **When to use:** Multi-room venues, private events, packages, add-on and pre-order sales, any operator who learns the night's mix only at the door.
- **How to score:** 5 if every revenue stream can be bought ahead and the operator knows the mix before service. 1 if guests discover the options only after arriving.
- **Fit:** Strong for venues with several offers under one roof. A single-format counter shop has little to expose.
- **Quote:** "people can't buy what you aren't selling" (S004)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S004, S005, S010/S011, S017)

## 7. Stranger danger (own the guest)
- **What it is:** You must know who your customers are. Capture the email as the unique identifier, then remarket and build lookalike lists. A platform that holds your guest data, charges per diner for guests you already had, or sends your overflow to a rival owns your business.
- **Parts:** Own guest data and CRM (S004, S011). Never sell a restaurant's excess demand to the competitor down the street (S010). Phones at the host stand are bad hospitality; know the guest before they walk in (S006). OpenTable would not share data and charged per diner even for returning or Google-sourced guests (S001, S012).
- **When to use:** Choosing a reservation, ordering, or delivery platform; loyalty and email programs.
- **How to score:** High if the operator owns the list and can reach guests directly. Low if the platform owns the relationship.
- **Fit:** He built a competitor to OpenTable; S010 to S012 are self-interested essays and he says so (S010). Weight the principle, discount the attacks.
- **Quote:** "Your customers cannot be strangers." (S004)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S004, S006, S010/S011, S012)

## 8. Follow the platform's money (the fee must fit the channel) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Read how a platform makes money before signing, and match the fee to the channel. Percent-of-gross fits food leaving the back door; it does not fit a dining room seat.
- **Parts:** Third-party delivery at 20 to 30 percent was tolerable as supplemental income and egregious when it became the only revenue (S002). His own channel ran at a flat 3 percent, with the diner paying $1 for pickup or a flat $5 to $7 for delivery (S002, S004). Network test for a competitor: real clients times plausible fee, against capital burned; about 521 mid-tier clients at roughly $200 a month is only about $100k MRR (S011). A cheap SaaS fee cannot fund software, so the money is in payments (S005, S010). Card-present versus card-not-present rates are a lever (S004, S005).
- **When to use:** Delivery and takeout channel decisions, reservation and POS contracts, evaluating a vendor's pitch.
- **How to score:** High if the operator knows the effective fee per order and what share of the channel's guests were already theirs. Low if the platform's claimed "network" is taken on faith.
- **Fit:** He preferred pickup to delivery and was not a fan of last-mile economics (S005). His flat-fee alternative was his own product.
- **Quote:** "Charging a percentage of gross works for sending a burger out the back door of the kitchen, but it won’t work in the dining room." (S010)
- **Sources:** 5 (S002, S004, S005, S010/S011, S012)

## 9. Sell the experience, effect first *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** The business sells a moment, like a concert or a game; food is the vehicle. Design backwards: decide the feeling, then the method, then the presentation (the magician's order).
- **Parts:** Effect, method, presentation (S006). Plant something early and pay it off later; clear clutter so each thing means something (S006). Build tension and release it, as theater does (S018). Keep it fun and social, not a temple; his repeated message was "fun and delicious" (S004, S007). Stand on the guest's side of the table and help them feel smart (S006).
- **When to use:** Concept design, special events, service training, justifying a price above the food cost.
- **How to score:** High if the founder can say in one page what it feels like to be there (his one-page diner test, S004). Low if the pitch is the menu.
- **Fit:** Built on a $300-a-head destination where each night is someone's big night (S007). At a neighborhood spot the experience is familiarity and speed more than theater. Inferred from S007, S009.
- **Quote:** "what we really did was sell experience" (S009)
- **Sources:** 5 (S004, S006, S007, S009, S018)

## 10. Asymmetric risk
- **What it is:** His main decision model from options trading. Take bets where the upside is a multiple of the downside, expect to be wrong often, and judge the pattern of decisions, not one outcome.
- **Parts:** Upside about 3 to 4 times downside, admitted to be hard to quantify (S001). Make many decisions with outcomes and be right about 51 percent of the time; be the house (S004). Hedge a small-probability existential risk even at 90 to 95 percent odds it will not happen (S002). Cut losers fast; doing nothing is a position (S002). Do not gamble without an edge (S009).
- **When to use:** New concepts, expansions, investments, crisis planning, killing a product.
- **How to score:** Guidance: ask for the realistic downside and upside in dollars. Near 3 to 1 or better with a capped downside scores high; a bet whose downside closes the business scores low however attractive the upside.
- **Fit:** Volume is what makes being wrong often affordable. An operator with one location gets few iterations, so a single bad bet weighs more. Inferred from S001, S004.
- **Quote:** "Always look for something that the upside is 3-4x the downside." (S001); "doing nothing is a choice" (S002)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S002, S004, S009)

## 11. Three shoeboxes (money in, money out, what is left)
- **What it is:** His father's method: one box for money in, one for money out, and what is left is profit. Work in dollars, not percentages, and keep cash ahead of obligations.
- **Parts:** Every model needs a tab for money in, money out, and what is left; if the third box is empty, change what you offer (S002). Look at spend by dollars, biggest items first (S001). Hold reserves in good years and never let payables depend on next week's sales (S002). Prepay concentrated vendors for a discount, roughly $220,000 for a quarter's exclusive (S002); he says prepaying purveyors with a reserve can move food cost from about 34 to about 24 percent (S005). Gift cards are liabilities, not rescue money (S002).
- **When to use:** Cash planning, vendor negotiations, crisis response, gift card and fundraising pushes.
- **How to score:** High if the operator can fund a vendor prepay from reserve and still cover a couple of months of big vendors. Low if this week's sales pay last month's bills (S004).
- **Fit:** His vendor prepay was funded by float from prepaid tickets (S004). An operator without that float has to fund it from savings, which changes the math. Inferred from S004, S005.
- **Quote:** "You can’t take percentages to the bank." (S001); "If you can’t pay for it, you can’t afford it." (S002)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S002, S004, S005)

## 12. First-principles spreadsheet (the Universal Restaurant Calculator)
- **What it is:** Build what the business should earn if everything ran perfectly, compare it to what you see, and attack the gap. Size any concept on an envelope before building.
- **Parts:** Inputs: covers per night, food and beverage check averages, food and beverage cost, labor, insurance, rent (S001). He says it modelled other restaurants within roughly 2 to 3 percent from two restaurants' real data (S001). Throughput times ticket times days, then variable costs, before the story (S006). A plan fits on one page or he does not understand it (S004). Walk away from an idea that fails the math (S009).
- **When to use:** New concepts, pop-ups, carryout pivots, any plan pitched on story.
- **How to score:** Guidance: he set a floor for one large concept (should not gross under $20M at roughly a $120 ticket, S006) and warned that pop-ups often lose money on production cost (S006). Score high if the founder's envelope covers costs at realistic throughput; low if the name and logo came before the math (his "time machine" plan, S001).
- **Fit:** Transfers well; the specific thresholds are for large-format concepts.
- **Quote:** "This will be run as a business first. It's not an art project." (S004); "There’s no point in looking at the dimes if you’ve got something that costs $1,000. Start there." (S001)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S004, S006, S009)

## 13. Attribution test (awards are not a business) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Separate prestige from measurable new customers. Ask every guest where they came from and spend on what brings them.
- **Parts:** Ask where each diner came from (S007). Michelin brought a week of news and no change in business; a Netflix feature brought 20 to 30 percent of diners on a given day (S007). No PR firm in 14 and a half years; measure every channel (S001). Talk to customers directly by email (S004). Distrust inflated press numbers (S010).
- **When to use:** PR spend, award chasing, influencer and media deals, marketing budgets.
- **How to score:** High if the operator can attribute covers to channels. Low if spend rests on reputation alone.
- **Fit:** His media lift came from national exposure a neighborhood operator rarely gets; the transferable part is the habit of asking. Inferred from S007.
- **Quote:** "It’s just a list." (S007); "nothing changes in terms of your actual business" (S007)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S004, S007, S010/S011)
