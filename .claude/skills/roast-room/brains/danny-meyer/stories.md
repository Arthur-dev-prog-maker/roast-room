# Stories: Danny Meyer

Built from the four extract batches over 24 sources (S001 to S024). 18 stories and cases he reuses, most-told first, with the lesson he draws. Source ids in parentheses.

1. **The hot dog cart that became Shake Shack.** In 2001 a cart run for an art installation in Madison Square Park drew lines of about 100; it lost money at first, made $7,500 by 2003, and became a kiosk in 2004 with the park as landlord. He built it to test "Can hospitality actually be expressed in something as mundane as a hot dog cart?" (S004). Lesson: test a hospitality idea cheaply, serve the purpose, and success can follow by accident (S003, S004, S010, S011, S016, S017, S024).
2. **The overcooked salmon at Gramercy Tavern.** A Union Square Cafe regular got her salmon well done, the GM left it on the bill, and she said it never would have happened at Union Square Cafe. The next day he held an all-staff meeting and named enlightened hospitality. "She gave us a gift by complaining." (S017). Lesson: you cannot lead by example in two places, so use your words (S004, S009, S016, S017, S018).
3. **Ten years to open a second restaurant.** His father went bankrupt twice, which he blamed on growth, so he set three tests for a second place that he thought could never be met. Therapy, Tom Colicchio asking to partner, and his wife Audrey got him to open Gramercy Tavern, and he failed all three tests that year. Lesson: the cause was his father's wrong team, not scale (S001, S003, S004, S008, S016, S020).
4. **Randy Garutti and the second Shack.** Garutti argued for about three years that the long line at the first Shack was the reason to open another. Both lines got longer. Lesson: demand, not scarcity, is the signal, and "Who am I to hold back more of that?" (S009) (S004, S008, S009, S024).
5. **Uncle Richard and the LSAT.** The night before law school exams, at Elio's, his uncle told him he would be dead a long time and should open the restaurant he always talked about. Lesson: follow the thing you cannot stop talking about (S001, S003, S004, S015, S019).
6. **Rome tour guide.** At 20 he made a game of turning the grumpiest tourist into the happiest by day four, and steered groups to family trattorias. Lesson: reading people is the core skill (S001, S002, S015).
7. **Closing Tabla.** He kept the 283-seat restaurant open about two years too long, then closed it with notice to staff, job fairs, landlord paid and investors repaid. "I kept it open for two years longer than I should have." (S001). Lesson: closing well can distinguish you as much as opening well (S001, S010, S024).
8. **The March 2020 layoffs.** Revenue went to zero, and about 2,000 of 2,200 staff were laid off in a few days, with benefits kept, a relief fund seeded by his salary and gift card sales, and later rehiring. He called it business chemotherapy: "in order to be alive, you have to almost kill yourself." (S005). Lesson: put people first even inside a layoff, and plan who you want to be on the other side (S005, S006, S007, S017, S021, S024).
9. **Hospitality Included and the sidewalk cash.** He ended tipping in 2015 to close the cook and server pay gap, piloting at The Modern. In summer 2020 sidewalk guests threw $20 to $100 bills at servers he had to tell to refuse them, and he brought tips back with a revenue share for cooks. Lesson: do the right thing at real cost, but the people you serve can overrule the model (S001, S010, S019, S023).
10. **The baby due date exception.** During the layoffs an employee's due date fell just after her health coverage ended, and he made an exception through the relief fund. "I may regret having an exception to what we’re doing, but I will never regret doing the right thing for that person." (S005). Lesson: a policy can bend for one person (S005).
11. **The 1990 smoking ban.** After his father died of lung cancer he banned smoking at Union Square Cafe; people said he would go out of business. "And we only got busier." (S019). Lesson: take strength from past contrarian calls (S010, S019).
12. **Calzones at Marta.** Thin-crust pizza did not survive delivery and the sidewalk faced a construction site, so he shut both and made a calzone virtual brand in the same kitchen, which stayed on the menu. Lesson: ask who wrote the rule (S006).
13. **9/11, closed for business, open for community.** His teams made sandwiches and ran a makeshift kitchen with no instruction from him, then the industry ran a cheap-meal promotion he called "CPR". Lesson: real culture acts when no one is looking (S020).
14. **Losing Union Square Cafe's lease.** After 30 years the rent was set to triple and he moved the restaurant. Lesson: landlords can push out independents, and a classic restaurant is one that outlives its original lease (S010, S024).
15. **Shake Shack in New Haven.** He sent a team to eat at local favorites months before opening and built walls from Yale Bowl wood. Lesson: 80 percent the same, 20 percent local, starting with humility (S002, S011).
16. **The busy Chicago Shack.** He saw water on the floor and ketchup on the counter, opened with a charitable assumption about a busy day, and learned there had been a big event. Lesson: micro care, do not play gotcha (S009).
17. **Eleven Madison Park and Tabla four weeks apart.** Opening two restaurants weeks apart was "the hardest thing I ever did" and he managed it poorly; Joyce Goldstein wrote that growth is like repotting a plant. Lesson: growth hurts before it works, and one at a time is safer (S012, S013).
18. **The 2009 two-star review.** Union Square Cafe dropped to two stars in the Times, and the third star took five years to win back. Lesson: a bad review now hurts team morale and talent more than revenue (S016, S024).
