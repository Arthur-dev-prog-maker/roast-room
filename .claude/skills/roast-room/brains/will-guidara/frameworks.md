# Frameworks: Will Guidara

Built from notes on 13 public sources (2022 to 2026), with quotes checked against the live pages.

14 frameworks, most-used first. The Sources line counts distinct sources where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds and examples, not quotes. Most of his examples come from Eleven Madison Park (EMP) and NoMad, which were fine dining; the Operator note lines flag where that fits a casual bar or counter-service shop badly.

---

## 1. Service versus hospitality (black and white versus color)
- **What it is:** Service is the product promise: right plate, right person, right time, right price, done efficiently. Hospitality is how the guest feels while that happens: seen, welcome, connected. He calls separating the two the biggest step toward his kind of culture.
- **Parts:** Service = what you do, judged on accuracy and speed. Hospitality = how you make people feel, judged on whether they feel seen. His test for a dining room: do two guests feel more connected at the end of the meal than at the start (S015).
- **When to use:** Any guest-experience decision. Also as an interview question: he asked every hire the difference (S015, S018).
- **How to score:** Guidance: high if the plan names a specific feeling for a specific guest and the basic promise already runs clean; middling if it improves only service (speed, accuracy); low if it is pure service talk dressed as hospitality, or hospitality on top of broken service.
- **Quote:** "service is black and white. Hospitality is color." (S002) "Service is simply a part of the product you’re selling." (S011)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S003, S011, S014, S015, S016, S018)

## 2. One size fits one (and the all, some, one ladder)
- **What it is:** The best gestures are bespoke to the person. In the newer version (2026) it is a ladder: all = raise an ordinary touchpoint for everyone; some = plan in advance for groups who share a moment; one = a singular gesture for one person.
- **Parts:** All (the cognac left with the check). Some (Chewy flowers after a pet dies, Tiffany flutes for engagements). One (the hot dog, the sleds for a family who had never seen snow). Only when it fits: read whether the guest wants engagement or wants you out of the way (business meeting, a bad first date, a couple fighting).
- **When to use:** Deciding what kind of gesture to build and for whom. The ladder is the version most useful to a casual or high-volume operator, because "all" and "some" can be systemized.
- **How to score:** Guidance: score whether the plan has a move at each rung. A plan that is only "one" depends on heroics and will not repeat; a plan that is only "all" is a policy, not hospitality.
- **Quote:** "Unreasonable hospitality is one size fits one. The greatest gestures are bespoke to the person receiving them." (S004) "one size fits all, one size fits some and one size fits one" (S019) "come up with one size fits one when it fits" (S002)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S004, S007, S011, S015, S017, S019)

## 3. Dreamweaver
- **What it is:** A dedicated person on the floor with no operational duties, whose only job is to help any team member turn a hospitality idea into reality. Without it, busy staff talk themselves out of good ideas.
- **Parts:** Front-line staff notice and originate ideas; the Dreamweaver executes and improves them. Hiring one to invent all the ideas fails (S004). He says companies outside restaurants (retirement homes, lawn care, banks, law offices) have copied the role (S014).
- **When to use:** When staff have ideas that die for lack of time or money, or when gestures happen only when the owner is present.
- **How to score:** Guidance: high if someone owns execution and has a small budget; low if the plan is a speech asking staff to go above and beyond with no resource behind it.
- **Operator note:** A full-time no-duty role was affordable at a high-check fine-dining room. For a casual shop the transferable part is the function (one named person per shift with time and a small budget for gestures), not the headcount (inferred from S001, S007, S014).
- **Quote:** "It was effectively like turning salespeople into product designers." (S002)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S002, S004, S007, S014, S017, S019)

## 4. Systemize the magic (go to the tapes, recurring moments) *(unnamed as a whole)*
- **What it is:** Do not wait for warm people to be kind. After a win, replay it to find what worked, then put a system behind it so it repeats. List the moments that recur in your business, decide the best response in advance, and build the assets to deliver it every time.
- **Parts:** Go to the tapes after a win (S002, S004). Map recurring moments, from daily to every few weeks (S017, S019). Pre-build the response: heated coats moved to a warm room when a table turns green on the host screen (S019); a stock of Tiffany boxes for engagements (S017). The hot dog one-off became a repeatable program (S014).
- **When to use:** Keeping a feeling consistent across shifts, managers, and locations. The most useful framework for a multi-unit or casual operator.
- **How to score:** Guidance: ask whether the gesture survives the best employee's day off. High if it is triggered by a system; low if it depends on one passionate person.
- **Quote:** "Nothing we did was hard. We were just willing to try harder." (S001)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S002, S004, S014, S017, S019)

## 5. Rule of 95/5
- **What it is:** Manage every resource, money and time, like a maniac 95% of the time, so you earn the right to spend the other 5% "foolishly" on gestures that build loyalty. He frames the 5% as the boundary that keeps gestures sustainable.
- **Parts:** The 95: tight cost control (his example is cutting paper towels in half). The 5: Dreamweavers, sleds, staff parties, a whiskey barrel in the basement. The 5% must go to staff as well as guests, or staff ask what is in it for them (S004). In bad times, tighten defense but keep playing offense with creativity when cash is short (S016, S018).
- **When to use:** Comp budgets, generosity line items, deciding whether to cut guest experience in a downturn.
- **How to score:** Guidance from his own numbers: the generosity slice should be small and deliberate (S003 cites 1% to 5%); score low both for open-ended comping and for a plan with zero gesture budget, which he calls reckless. A business with sloppy costs has not earned the 5%.
- **Quote:** "I manage every expense like a freaking maniac 95% of the time." (S004) "if you are evenly distributing your focus on expenses, you’re going to have an efficient but completely unmemorable business." (S004) "no team, when they are losing, ever wins if they only play defense" (S016)
- **Sources:** 5 (S003, S004, S014, S016, S019), with the adversity rule also in S018

## 6. Excellence is table stakes
- **What it is:** Never talk about hospitality without excellence, and never talk about excellence without hospitality. A well-run machine is the prerequisite; it is only the baseline promise, not the goal. Unreasonable hospitality means being as relentless about how people feel as successful businesses already are about their product.
- **Parts:** Fix the operation first. Then add the human layer. He admits he started by chasing excellence only and at first did nothing memorable (S002).
- **When to use:** Any proposal to add surprise gestures. Ask first whether tickets are right, food is on time, and the room is clean.
- **How to score:** Guidance: cap the score if the basics are failing. Gestures on a broken operation are lipstick.
- **Quote:** "my entire thesis is that excellence is merely table stakes." (S001)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S002, S014, S015, S017)

## 7. Be present, notice, act (the hot dog)
- **What it is:** His three ingredients from the hot dog story: be present (care about the person so much you stop caring about the rest of your list); take the work seriously but not yourself; one size fits one. Overhear the unmet wish, then deliver it fast and individually.
- **Parts:** Listen to what guests say and do not say (S007). Pick the detail worth acting on. Match the gesture to the person: a two-dollar hot dog beat champagne and caviar.
- **When to use:** Training staff to spot moments; judging whether a gesture is specific enough.
- **How to score:** Guidance: high if the gesture could only have been given to this guest; low if it is a generic comp (a bottle, a dessert) that lacks specificity.
- **Quote:** "taking what you do seriously without taking yourself too seriously" (S007) "it’s not the cost of the gesture that matters. It’s how it makes people feel." (S007)
- **Sources:** 5 (S002, S004, S007, S014, S015)

## 8. Team first (hospitality is a team sport)
- **What it is:** From Danny Meyer. Invest in staff the way you want them to invest in guests, because people cannot give a feeling they have not received. He argues it as strategy: he could personally touch maybe 4% of guests but 100% of staff, who pass it on (S011).
- **Parts:** Give staff the experience first (his staff dining gestures were "far crazier" than guests', S014). Prime the pump by being nice to your people (S004). Fix morale causes rather than scheduling a happy hour (S015).
- **When to use:** Culture, retention, or any plan that asks staff to deliver more warmth.
- **How to score:** Guidance: low if the plan asks the front line to give more while giving them nothing; high if staff get the same kind of care first.
- **Quote:** "I don't think it's possible to know how good it feels to give it until you first have experienced how good it feels to receive it." (S014)
- **Sources:** 5 (S004, S011, S014, S015, S016)

## 9. Touchpoint audit (find the overlooked, transactional moment)
- **What it is:** List every touchpoint of the guest journey, including unlikely ones (the wait, the bathroom, the bill, the unsubscribe note). Find what feels transactional and the one nobody else considered, and make it a little more awesome. He calls that strategy.
- **Parts:** Walk the journey. Flag transactional moments. Spend creativity on dead moments. His examples: Five Guys peanuts while waiting (S004); the cognac check drop so the big bill meets a generous gesture, cheap because most pour one splash (S017); how you say goodbye (S019).
- **When to use:** Low-budget guest-experience upgrades. This and framework 4 port best to casual operations.
- **How to score:** Guidance: high for a small upgrade on a moment every guest hits and competitors ignore; low for spending on features guests do not remember (he cites hotel thread counts and soap, S011).
- **Quote:** "feeling of transaction is one of the enemies of hospitality" (S002) "how you say goodbye is just as important as how you say hello" (S019)
- **Sources:** 5 (S002, S004, S011, S017, S019)

## 10. Hiring filter (right people, not best people)
- **What it is:** A conversational interview, run like a first date, looking for integrity and passion. Four questions: can I trust them, will they work hard, do I want to spend time with them, will my people want to spend time with them (S001).
- **Parts:** Keep must-have credentials short so you can interview the most people (S001). Ask the difference between service and hospitality (S015, S018). Do not trust charm; a shy person may be more hospitable (S001). Hire entry level and require a couple of seasons before someone becomes a server (S001).
- **When to use:** Any hiring decision, especially front-of-house.
- **How to score:** Guidance: high if the process tests chemistry and care; low if it filters on resume and experience only or runs a scripted list.
- **Operator note:** The seasons-before-you-serve ladder assumes low turnover and a deep bench. In a high-turnover casual shop the filter ports; the ladder may not (inferred from S001, S005).
- **Quote:** "We over-index on trying to hire for capacity and under-index on trying to hire for chemistry." (S001) "I try to pick the right people, not the best people" (S004)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S004, S015, S017, S018)

## 11. Language and repetition build culture (isms, titles, pre-meal)
- **What it is:** Culture is a living thing that must be tended. Write down values and non-negotiables, give them short phrases (isms), rename roles to imply behavior, and repeat them daily in the 30-minute pre-service meeting until you are sick of hearing yourself.
- **Parts:** Isms from Danny Meyer: perception is reality, be the swan, constant gentle pressure (S017, S018). Titles: cafeteria cashier became Menu Consultant at MoMA (S003, S017). Pre-meal covers what, how, and why, with outside inspiration; staff take turns leading it (S001). Two buckets: inspiration of the week, and the permanent collection of non-negotiables (S001). All-hands strategy days where small groups own projects (S017).
- **When to use:** Building or repairing culture; scaling a feeling across shifts.
- **How to score:** Guidance: high if values are named, short, and repeated daily by people other than the owner; low if they live on a poster.
- **Quote:** "the idea that 30 minutes a day can transform a culture" (S018) "It needs to be tended to and fed and watered and nurtured." (S018)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S003, S004, S014, S016, S017, S018)

## 12. Rules of praise and criticism
- **What it is:** Criticize in private, praise in public; criticize the behavior, not the person; do it consistently, not by mood; keep emotion out. Then tailor delivery to the person (tough love languages). Give hundreds of small corrections a night, not only annual reviews.
- **Parts:** Charitable assumption first (ask if something is wrong before addressing repeated lateness, but always address it, S011). If you delivered it badly, apologize next day for the delivery, not the message (S011). Praise as often as you correct, because leaders see what is wrong first (S017). Managers read The One Minute Manager (S017).
- **When to use:** Manager training, performance problems, feedback culture.
- **How to score:** Guidance: high if feedback is frequent, private, and specific; low if it is saved for reviews or delivered angry.
- **Quote:** "Criticize the behavior, not the person." (S011) "praise is affirmation. Criticism is investment." (S011)
- **Sources:** 3 (S011, S016, S017)

## 13. Restaurant smart versus corporate smart (excellence versus hospitality tension)
- **What it is:** Corporate smart is control: profitable but not special. Restaurant smart is creativity: caring but financially fragile. Aim for both. Excellence and hospitality are not friends, in his telling: fear yields precision without humanity, all praise yields warmth with sloppy detail. Success lives in the tension.
- **Parts:** Control what should be controlled; let go where staff know the guest better than the leader does (S017).
- **When to use:** Balancing standards against staff autonomy; deciding whether a new process will kill warmth.
- **How to score:** Guidance: low for plans at either extreme; high when the plan says which parts are fixed and which are left to staff judgment.
- **Quote:** "Restaurant smart versus corporate smart is effectively control versus creativity." (S003)
- **Sources:** 2 (S003, S017)

## 14. Idea pressure tests (do not ruin a story with the facts, red team)
- **What it is:** Two moves held together. First decide what you want, then figure out how; do not let facts kill an exciting idea early. Then, before changing something that already works, assign a red team to argue it is bad, and keep asking how it can be better until nothing is left to improve.
- **Parts:** Do not ruin a story with the facts (S017, S019). Red team before reinventing a signature item; the NoMad chicken survived with a wishbone added (S017). Pressure-test until no marginal improvement remains (S003). Schedule creativity in a 90-minute meeting on a named problem (S017). Never shut down a new hire's idea because it failed before; his car service idea took seven years (S017).
- **When to use:** New guest-experience ideas, menu or format changes, or tweaks to a signature item.
- **How to score:** Guidance: new ideas get room to breathe; changes to something that already works must survive a red team first.
- **Quote:** "do not ruin a story with the facts" (S017)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S017, S019)
